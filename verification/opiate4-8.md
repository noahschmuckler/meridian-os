# Bundle 4
Below is a claim-by-claim verification against current evidence and guidelines.

---

**Claim 1: POC immunoassay UDS has meaningful false-positive and false-negative rates and should not be the basis for clinical action against a patient.**

**Supported.** The CDC 2022 Guideline (Recommendation 10) explicitly states that "false positive and false negative presumptive results are not uncommon" and that this problem is "compounded because clinicians commonly misinterpret results, leading to inappropriate consequences for patients".[1] Analytically, POC immunoassays demonstrate suboptimal positive predictive values — as low as 57% for oxycodone on some platforms — and poor negative predictive values for several drug classes (e.g., 25–29% for THC-COOH) when compared to LC-MS/MS.[2] A 2026 review of false-positive interferences across 61 studies confirmed that immunoassay screens remain presumptive and require confirmation by GC-MS or LC-MS/MS.[3] The AAFP similarly states that "all results are considered presumptive until confirmatory testing is performed".[4]

One nuance: the claim as stated ("should not be the basis for clinical action against a patient") is correct for adverse/punitive actions (discharge, termination, dose reduction). However, POC results can appropriately inform preliminary safety decisions such as withholding a same-day prescription pending confirmation, increasing monitoring frequency, or initiating a conversation — these are not "actions against" the patient.[5][1] The operational distinction for the PCP is: a screening result can trigger a clinical conversation and a confirmatory send-out, but should not trigger discharge, termination, or documentation of a "violation."

---

**Claim 2: Confirmation with GC-MS or LC-MS/MS is required before discharge, termination, or other adverse clinical action based on a screening UDS.**

**Supported with important qualification.** The CDC 2022 Guideline states confirmatory testing "should be used when toxicology results will inform decisions with major clinical or nonclinical implications for the patient".[1] The ASAM guideline similarly reserves confirmatory testing for resolving unexpected screening results.[6] The ACMT position statement reinforces that specific confirmatory assays are needed to differentiate individual opioids and metabolites.[7]

The qualification: the CDC 2022 Guideline goes further than just requiring confirmation — it states that **clinicians should not dismiss patients from care on the basis of a toxicology test result at all**, even a confirmed one.[1] Dismissal "could have adverse consequences for patient safety, potentially including the patient obtaining opioids from alternative sources and the clinician missing opportunities to facilitate treatment for substance use disorder".[1] So the claim is correct but understates the guideline position: confirmation is necessary but not sufficient for adverse action. Even confirmed unexpected results should prompt a clinical response (MOUD evaluation, increased monitoring, naloxone, referral) rather than termination.

---

**Claim 3: A UDS negative for the prescribed opiate suggests (a) diversion/stockpiling, (b) dose-timing artifact, or (c) metabolic variant.**

**Partially supported — the list is incomplete.** Published differential diagnoses for an absent prescribed opioid are broader. A JAMA review lists the following categories:[8]

- Drug is absent (not taken in past 72 hours): **diversion**, **hoarding/stockpiling**, or **pharmacologic induction causing rapid metabolism**
- Specimen manipulation: **dilution or adulteration** (including substitution of synthetic urine)
- Drug is present but below assay cutoff (dose-timing artifact, low-dose/PRN use)
- Wrong assay ordered (e.g., standard opiate immunoassay does not detect oxycodone, fentanyl, methadone, buprenorphine)
- Laboratory error (mislabeling, cross-reaction causing interference)

The HIVMA/IDSA guideline adds **urine dilution from uncontrolled diabetes mellitus** and **running out early due to undertreated pain** (the patient used more than prescribed and has none left) as distinct from simple diversion.[9] The AAPM consensus adds **pharmacogenetic anomalies** and **drug-drug interactions** affecting metabolism.[10]

Operationally, the PCP should document consideration of all of these possibilities. "Metabolic variant" is real but rare; **wrong assay ordered** and **running out early** are far more common explanations that the module should include. Notably, the NCCN table highlights that patients taking opioids PRN may have negative results based on infrequent use patterns outside the detection window.[11]

---

**Claim 4: Risk-stratified UDS cadence — stable every 6–12 months, yellow-flag every 3 months, active concerns at every visit.**

**Partially supported — this is a reasonable institutional convention, not a specific guideline recommendation.** The CDC 2016 Guideline recommended UDS "at least annually" for all patients on chronic opioid therapy but did not risk-stratify the UDS interval.[12] The CDC 2022 Guideline softened this to "periodically (at least annually)" and explicitly noted that "predicting risk is challenging, and available tools do not allow clinicians to reliably identify patients who are at low risk" — the CDC deliberately declined to recommend risk-stratified UDS cadence.[1]

The AAPM consensus (Argoff et al. 2018) does endorse risk-stratified monitoring and summarizes existing guidelines as recommending every 6 months to 2 years for low-risk, 1–3 times per year for moderate-risk, and 2–4 times per year for high-risk patients.[10] The American Pain Society (2009) suggested that low-risk stable patients might be monitored every 3–6 months.[13] These ranges are broadly consistent with the module's cadence but are not identical to the specific "6–12 / 3 / every visit" tiers.

The PCP should document the rationale for the chosen interval. The module's cadence is defensible but should be framed as institutional policy informed by expert consensus, not as a specific CDC or ASAM recommendation.

---

**Claim 5: The PCP should document the reasoning behind the chosen UDS interval.**

**Supported.** The AAPM consensus recommends that "a summary of communications and discussions about results with the laboratory and other experts can be included in the medical record to document the medical necessity of testing and related clinical decision-making".[10] The CDC 2022 Guideline emphasizes that clinicians should "have a plan for responding to unexpected results" before ordering testing.[1] CMS and payer policies require documentation of medical necessity for definitive testing.[10] Documentation of the risk assessment and monitoring rationale is standard of care for medicolegal protection and is consistent with all major guidelines.

---

**Claim 6: Non-accusatory framing is the appropriate response to an unexpected UDS result.**

**Strongly supported.** The CDC 2022 Guideline states clinicians should "explain to patients that toxicology testing will not be used punitively (e.g., will not be used to dismiss patients from care) and is intended to improve their safety" and should "ask patients about use of prescribed medications and other substances and ask whether there might be unexpected results".[1] The AAPM consensus recommends that "communications with patients about the purpose and results of UDM should be nonjudgmental and nonpunitive and should focus on safety" and suggests asking "If we test you today, what will we find in your urine? Will there be any surprises?".[10] The AAFP summary of the CDC 2022 Guideline similarly states that "results should not be used in a punitive manner".[14]

The specific phrasing in the module ("I want to make sure we understand this result together before drawing any conclusions") is consistent with this guidance. The CDC 2022 Guideline also notes that "discussion with patients before specific confirmatory testing can sometimes yield a candid explanation of why a particular substance is present or absent and obviate the need for confirmatory testing on that visit".[1]

---

**Claim 7: Cocaine-positive UDS in a chronic opioid patient is an OUD evaluation trigger — consider MOUD rather than simply discontinuing the opioid.**

**Supported in principle, though the reasoning requires refinement.** The CDC 2022 Guideline (Recommendation 12) directs clinicians to "offer or arrange evidence-based treatment (usually medication-assisted treatment with buprenorphine or methadone in combination with behavioral therapies) for patients with opioid use disorder".[12] The guideline explicitly warns against dismissal from care and states that unexpected results should prompt the clinician to "offer or refer patients for substance use disorder treatment".[1] An NEJM review states that "opioid use disorder in patients with chronic pain is an indication for more care rather than less" and that "a punitive approach, such as dismissal from care, is counterproductive and places patients at greater risk for overdose if they transition from pharmaceutical opioids to illicit ones".[15]

Important nuance: **cocaine use does not by itself diagnose OUD** — it may indicate a stimulant use disorder, polysubstance use, or co-occurring substance use disorders. The appropriate response is a comprehensive SUD evaluation (not just OUD screening), which may lead to MOUD if OUD criteria are met, but should also address the stimulant use disorder itself. Abrupt opioid discontinuation in this context is associated with increased overdose risk, particularly among patients with concurrent OUD.[16][1] The operational step is: confirm the result → conduct a structured SUD assessment → prescribe naloxone → consider MOUD if OUD criteria are met → do not reflexively discontinue the opioid prescription.

---

**Claim 8: Non-prescribed fentanyl positive is a high-acuity finding indicating illicit street-opioid use and is an OUD/MOUD conversation.**

**Supported, with critical technical caveats.** The CDC 2022 Guideline notes that "testing for fentanyl is not available in widely used toxicology assays, potentially leading to false assurance".[1] This means that if fentanyl is detected, it was likely tested for specifically (via a fentanyl-specific immunoassay or definitive testing), which increases the reliability of the result. In the current drug supply, non-prescribed fentanyl is overwhelmingly illicit, and its presence in a patient not prescribed fentanyl is a high-acuity finding consistent with illicit opioid exposure.

Caveats for the PCP:

- **Cross-contamination of the drug supply**: Fentanyl may be present in counterfeit pills or mixed into non-opioid drugs (cocaine, methamphetamine, benzodiazepines). The patient may not know they were exposed to fentanyl. This does not reduce the acuity of the finding but changes the counseling approach.
- **Confirm the result**: Standard immunoassay panels often do not include fentanyl; if detected on a POC cup, confirm with LC-MS/MS.[1]
- The clinical response should include naloxone prescribing, fentanyl test strip education, structured OUD assessment, and MOUD discussion — framed as a safety intervention, not a punitive one.[15][1]

The claim's characterization as "an OUD/MOUD conversation rather than a prescription continuation decision" is well-aligned with CDC guidance. Abrupt discontinuation in this scenario carries the highest overdose risk.[16][1][17]

---

**Summary of operational corrections for the decision-support tool:**

1. The differential for absent prescribed opioid should be expanded to include **wrong assay ordered**, **running out early/undertreated pain**, **specimen dilution/adulteration**, and **laboratory error** — not just diversion, timing, and metabolism.
2. The cocaine-positive scenario should trigger a **comprehensive SUD evaluation** (not just OUD screening), since cocaine use indicates stimulant use disorder that also requires attention.
3. The fentanyl-positive scenario should include counseling about **cross-contamination of the drug supply** — the patient may not have intentionally used an opioid.
4. The risk-stratified UDS cadence should be labeled as **institutional convention informed by expert consensus**, not attributed to a specific guideline. The CDC 2022 Guideline deliberately avoids risk-stratified UDS intervals.

# Bundle 5
Below is a claim-by-claim analysis with genuine pushback where warranted.

---

**Claim 1: Concurrent opioid + benzodiazepine use increases overdose mortality risk by a factor of 3–4 compared to opioids alone.**

**Partially supported but oversimplified.** The CDC 2022 guideline (Recommendation 11) cites a case-cohort study showing "a near-quadrupling of risk for overdose death" with concurrent benzodiazepine prescription compared to opioid prescription alone.[1] However, the broader literature shows a wide range of effect sizes depending on the outcome measured and the population studied. A large retrospective analysis of privately insured patients found an adjusted OR of **2.14** for opioid overdose-related ED visit or hospitalization.[2] A Kaiser Permanente study found only a **20% increase** in overdose risk for opioid + benzodiazepine users vs. opioid-only users.[3] A Medicare study found the risk is highly time-dependent: the hazard ratio was **5.05** in the first 90 days of concurrent use but dropped to non-significant levels after 180 days of stable concurrent use.[4]

**Operational correction:** The "3–4×" figure is defensible only when citing the specific case-cohort study referenced by the CDC for overdose *death*. For a decision-support tool, it would be more accurate to state that the risk increase ranges from roughly 2× to 5× depending on the population, outcome, dose, and duration, and that the risk is highest in the early period of concurrent use and at higher doses. Long-term, stable, low-dose concurrent use carries lower (though still elevated) risk.[4][1]

---

**Claim 2: Both drug classes cause respiratory depression through different mechanisms; the combination is synergistic, not merely additive.**

**Partially supported but the "synergistic" characterization is more nuanced than stated.** Opioids depress the ventilatory response to hypercapnia via mu-opioid receptors in brainstem respiratory centers. Benzodiazepines act on GABA-A receptors and, at standard doses, have **minimal effects on ventilation on their own** but can potentiate opioid-induced respiratory depression.[5] A 2025 FDA-sponsored RCT in healthy volunteers found that midazolam + oxycodone did *not* significantly decrease hypercapnic ventilatory response compared to oxycodone alone, but did increase resting end-tidal CO₂ and caused 60% of participants to fall asleep or snore (vs. 0% on oxycodone alone), suggesting the mechanism involves **upper airway obstruction and loss of protective reflexes** rather than purely additive central respiratory drive suppression.[6] Pharmacologically, the interaction through colocalized mu-opioid and GABA receptors has been described as synergistic in dopaminergic and respiratory pathways.[7]

**Operational correction:** The claim that the combination is "synergistic, not merely additive" is a reasonable clinical shorthand, but the precise mechanism is still being elucidated. The most current evidence suggests benzodiazepines primarily contribute to overdose risk by causing sedation, loss of airway protective reflexes, and upper airway obstruction rather than through a classic pharmacodynamic synergy on central respiratory drive.[6] For a decision-support tool, stating that "benzodiazepines potentiate opioid-induced respiratory depression through complementary mechanisms" is more accurate than claiming strict pharmacologic synergy.

---

**Claim 3: The FDA issued its black box warning on the opioid + benzodiazepine combination in 2016.**

**Correct.** In **2016**, the FDA required that drug labeling for both benzodiazepines and opioids include boxed warnings about the increased potential for respiratory depression with simultaneous use.[5] This is confirmed in current FDA labeling for both opioids (e.g., oxycodone) and benzodiazepines (e.g., lorazepam/Ativan).[8][9] No correction needed.

---

**Claim 4: An inherited patient on both opioids and a benzodiazepine cannot be continued on the unchanged combination at first visit without a documented safety plan and specialist input.**

**Overstated relative to guidelines.** No major guideline (CDC 2022, VA/DoD 2022, ASAM 2025) mandates that the combination *cannot* be continued at the first visit without specialist input. The CDC 2022 guideline (Recommendation 11) is a **Category B recommendation** — meaning "use particular caution" rather than "avoid" — and explicitly acknowledges that "in specific situations, benzodiazepines can be beneficial, and stopping benzodiazepines can be destabilizing" and that "long-term, stable use might be safer than erratic, unpredictable use".[1] The guideline recommends consultation with specialists "if needed" and "as appropriate," not as a prerequisite for continuing prescriptions.[1]

The ASAM 2025 Joint Guideline on Benzodiazepine Tapering recommends risk-benefit assessment at least every 3 months and prescribing naloxone, but does not require specialist input before continuing the combination.[10]

**Operational correction:** At the first visit, the PCP *can* continue both medications at current doses if the patient is on a long-term, stable regimen, provided the following are documented:

1. PDMP check performed
2. Risk-benefit assessment documented (including dose, duration, other risk factors)
3. Naloxone prescribed or offered[10]
4. Plan for follow-up within a shorter interval (e.g., 2–4 weeks rather than 3 months) given the high-risk regimen[1]
5. Specialist consultation *initiated* (not necessarily completed) if there is concern about the indication for either medication or if tapering is being considered[1]

Refusing to prescribe both at the first visit risks destabilizing the patient and is explicitly cautioned against by the CDC 2022 guideline's expert working group.[1]

---

**Claim 5: When the inherited combination has a legitimate dual indication, the appropriate first-visit move is to prescribe whichever is more immediately necessary (typically the opioid), document the combination as a known risk, prescribe naloxone, and initiate the e-consult urgently.**

**Partially supported but the "prescribe only one" framing is problematic.** No guideline recommends withholding one of two chronically prescribed medications at the first visit when the patient has legitimate indications for both and is on a stable regimen. The CDC 2022 guideline explicitly warns against abrupt changes that could destabilize patients.[1] The VA/DoD 2022 guideline similarly states that abrupt discontinuation of benzodiazepines should be avoided due to risk of seizures and death.[11]

The naloxone prescription and documentation of risk are well-supported: the ASAM 2025 guideline makes prescribing naloxone a **strong recommendation** for all patients co-prescribed opioids and benzodiazepines.[10] Initiating specialist consultation is reasonable and supported.

**Operational correction:** At the first visit, the PCP should **continue both medications at current doses** (not choose one), document the combination as a known risk, prescribe naloxone, check the PDMP, and initiate the specialist referral. Withholding the benzodiazepine risks withdrawal seizure; withholding the opioid risks withdrawal and uncontrolled pain. The decision-support tool should frame this as "bridge both, document, and refer" rather than "choose one."

---

**Claim 6: Specialist input — psychiatry for the benzo or pain management for the opioid — is required before the PCP owns both prescriptions on an ongoing basis.**

**Not supported as a hard requirement by any major guideline.** The CDC 2022 guideline recommends that clinicians "communicate with mental health professionals managing the patient" and consider "consultation with other specialists (e.g., an addiction specialist) if needed".[1] The VA/DoD 2022 guideline similarly recommends providers "consider obtaining a specialty consultation, as appropriate".[11] The ASAM 2025 guideline recommends coordinating care with other prescribers but does not mandate specialist sign-off.[12]

**Operational correction:** Specialist consultation is **strongly recommended** but not a prerequisite for ongoing PCP management. Many PCPs manage both prescriptions, particularly in settings with limited specialist access. The decision-support tool should frame this as: "Specialist input is strongly recommended and should be actively pursued, but the PCP may continue managing both prescriptions with appropriate documentation of risk-benefit assessment, naloxone provision, PDMP monitoring, and regular reassessment at least every 3 months".[10][1] State-level regulations (e.g., NY I-STOP) may impose additional requirements — these should be verified separately as they vary by jurisdiction.

---

**Claim 7: Benzodiazepine taper in a chronically dependent patient should not be initiated unilaterally at first visit because abrupt reduction risks withdrawal seizure.**

**Strongly supported.** The CDC 2022 guideline states that "clinicians should taper benzodiazepines gradually before discontinuation because abrupt withdrawal can be associated with rebound anxiety, hallucinations, seizures, delirium tremens, and, rarely, death".[1] The ASAM 2025 Joint Guideline emphasizes that "clinicians should not discontinue BZDs abruptly in patients who are likely to be physically dependent and at risk of withdrawal" and that tapering should be individualized based on patient response.[13] The NEJM review on benzodiazepine dependence recommends gradual discontinuation over 4–8 weeks minimum, with hospitalization considered for very high doses (≥100 mg diazepam equivalent daily).[14]

“In patients receiving opioid maintenance therapy, the dose of the opioid (e.g., methadone) should be kept stable throughout the benzodiazepine-reduction period and high enough to prevent symptoms of opioid withdrawal… Concurrent opioid detoxification is not recommended.”
— Michael Soyka, M.D.Ludwig Maximilian University and other institutions

**Minor nuance:** The claim says "should not be initiated unilaterally at first visit," which is correct as a practical matter, but the reason is not just seizure risk — it is also that the first visit lacks the therapeutic relationship, baseline assessment, and coordination needed for safe tapering. The AAFP guidance emphasizes that successful deprescribing requires patient education, shared decision-making, and frequent dialogue.[15]

---

**Claim 8: The benzodiazepine taper plan should be coordinated with psychiatry and kept separate from the opioid management conversation so the patient is not facing two simultaneous tapers.**

**Partially supported with important nuance.** The principle of avoiding simultaneous tapers is well-supported. The NEJM review explicitly states that "concurrent opioid detoxification is not recommended" during benzodiazepine tapering, and that the opioid dose should be kept stable throughout the benzodiazepine reduction period.[14] The CDC 2022 guideline states that decisions about which medication to taper first "should be individualized" and lists options including tapering opioids first, tapering benzodiazepines first, or transitioning to buprenorphine before tapering benzodiazepines.[1] A 2023 scoping review found that among 26 guidelines addressing concurrent use, **zero** provided specific guidance on deprescribing sequence, and the 4 that addressed it gave **conflicting recommendations**.[16]

**Operational corrections:**

- Coordinating with psychiatry is recommended but not mandated — the CDC says to "communicate with mental health professionals managing the patient". If no psychiatrist is involved, the PCP or an addiction specialist can manage the taper.[1]
- The claim that the taper should be "kept separate from the opioid management conversation" is reasonable as a clinical strategy to avoid overwhelming the patient, but it is not a guideline recommendation per se. The CDC 2022 guideline notably shifted from the 2016 version (which suggested it "might be safer and more practical to taper opioids first") to a more individualized approach in 2022.[17][1]
- The ASAM 2025 guideline recommends that patients with OUD should be stabilized on MOUD *before* initiating a benzodiazepine taper, which is an important sequencing consideration not captured in the claim.[10][12]

**Claim 1: Continuity of an established opioid regimen at an inherited high-dose visit while arranging specialist oversight is clinically and ethically defensible; abrupt discontinuation or reduction at first visit is not.**

**Supported.** The CDC 2022 guideline (Recommendation 5) explicitly states that "opioid therapy should not be discontinued abruptly, and clinicians should not rapidly reduce opioid dosages from higher dosages" unless there are life-threatening signs such as impending overdose.[1] The guideline further warns that payers and health systems "should ensure that policies based on cautionary dosage thresholds do not result in rapid tapers or abrupt discontinuation of opioids" and should not "penalize clinicians for accepting new patients who are receiving opioids for chronic pain".[1] The FDA has separately advised that risks of rapid tapering or sudden discontinuation in physically dependent patients include acute withdrawal, pain exacerbation, serious psychological distress, and suicidal ideation.[1]

Nuance: Continuity is defensible only in the absence of **life-threatening safety signals** at the first visit (e.g., sedation, respiratory depression, slurred speech, evidence of active diversion). If such signals are present, dose reduction or discontinuation may be warranted even at the first encounter.[1] Additionally, "continuity" does not mean indefinite passive prescribing — the CDC 2022 guideline expects the new clinician to establish treatment goals, including functional goals, and to reassess within 3 months or sooner.[1] The claim should specify that continuity is a bridge strategy, not an endpoint.

---

**Claim 2: Abrupt taper of a long-stable high-dose patient causes harm — undertreated pain, withdrawal, loss of function — and in some cases drives patients to illicit opioids.**

**Supported, with strong evidence.** A large JAMA cohort study (Agnoli et al., 2021; n = 113,618) found that opioid dose tapering in patients on stable, long-term, higher-dose therapy was associated with significantly increased rates of **overdose** (adjusted IRR 1.68) and **mental health crisis** (adjusted IRR 2.28), including suicide attempts (adjusted IRR 3.30).[2] Critically, the risk was dose-dependent: patients tapering from ≥300 MME/day had the highest absolute risk increases.[2] The CDC 2022 guideline cites evidence that discontinuation of long-term, high-dose therapy has been associated with mental health crisis, overdose events, overdose death, and heroin use.[1] The HHS 2019 tapering guidance and the FDA safety communication both specifically warn about transition to illicit opioids.[2][1]

“In a large cohort of patients in the US prescribed stable, long-term, higher-dose opioids, undergoing opioid dose tapering was associated with statistically significant risk of subsequent overdose and mental health crisis, including suicidality.”
— Alicia Agnoli, MD, MPH, MHS1,2,  et al.Department of Family and Community Medicine, University of California, Davis, Sacramento and other institutions

Nuance: The evidence is strongest for **rapid** taper and **discontinuation**; the CDC 2022 guideline notes that when patients agree to taper and it is done gradually (≤10% per month) with behavioral support, outcomes including pain, function, and quality of life can actually improve.[1] The claim as written is accurate for abrupt taper but should not be read as evidence that all tapering is harmful. The AAFP summary notes that for patients on opioids ≥1 year, tapers can take months to years.[3]

**Claim 1: Early-refill threshold ladder (1 request → conversation; 2+ → formal warning + UDS + PDMP; 3+ → e-consult)**

**Verdict: Not supported by any published guideline as a specific threshold ladder. This is institutional convention, not evidence-based standard.**

No published guideline — including the CDC 2022 Opioid Prescribing Guideline, the VA/DoD 2022 guideline, the APS/AAPM 2009 guideline, or ASAM — specifies numeric thresholds for early-refill requests that trigger escalating interventions.[1][2][3] The general framework of graduated response to aberrant behaviors is well-supported, but the specific numbers (1, 2, 3) are fabricated thresholds.

What the evidence actually supports:

- The APS/AAPM 2009 guideline notes that "one or two episodes of unauthorized opioid escalations" in low-risk patients can often be managed with education and enhanced monitoring, while "repeatedly nonadherent" patients or those with "four or more previous aberrant drug-related behaviors" may require consultation, restructuring, or discontinuation. This is the closest published threshold, but it counts all aberrant behaviors (not just early refills) and uses 4+ as the inflection point for concern about substance use disorder.[2]
- The VA/DoD 2022 guideline lists "using more than prescribed and/or running out early" as a factor indicating need for more frequent follow-up, without specifying a count.[3]
- The CDC 2022 guideline deliberately avoids rigid thresholds and emphasizes individualized clinical judgment.[4]

**Operational correction:** The ladder is reasonable as an institutional protocol, but should be labeled as such. The tool should state: "This escalation framework is a practice-level convention; no published guideline specifies numeric early-refill thresholds." The PDMP review component is actually understated — under NY I-STOP, PDMP review is **mandatory before every controlled substance prescription**, not just at the 2+ threshold.[5][6] UDS should also be part of baseline monitoring for all patients on chronic opioid therapy, not triggered only by early refills.[7][8]

**Claim 2: Document each early-refill instance at the time it occurs because patterns are only visible in retrospect**

**Verdict: Supported as sound clinical practice, though not stated in exactly this form in any guideline.**

The APS/AAPM 2009 guideline recommends that monitoring "should include documentation of pain intensity and level of functioning, assessments of progress towards achieving therapeutic goals, presence of adverse events, and adherence to prescribed therapies".[2] The CDC 2016 and 2022 guidelines both emphasize ongoing documentation of benefits, harms, and adherence at each encounter.[1][7] The principle that real-time documentation of individual events enables retrospective pattern recognition is a documentation best practice rather than a guideline-specific recommendation, but it is fully consistent with the standard of care for controlled substance prescribing and with medicolegal documentation standards.[9]

**Operational note:** This claim is accurate and defensible. The ACOEM Opioid Treatment Contract template explicitly includes language about medication adherence monitoring.[10] Documentation should include the date of the request, the reason given, the clinical response, and any monitoring actions taken.

**Claim 3: Frequent between-visit contact around controlled substances (calls, portal messages requesting early refills, dose increases, exceptions) is itself a behavioral red flag**

**Verdict: Supported, with important nuance.**

Early refill requests and unsanctioned dose escalation requests are well-established as **aberrant drug-related behaviors** in the pain management literature.[11][12][13] The ASCO guideline explicitly categorizes "a pattern of early refills" as an aberrant behavior, though one on the less serious end of the spectrum.[11] The Delphi study by Merlin et al. (2018) identified "asking for an increase in opioid dose" and "using more opioid medication than prescribed" among the most common concerning behaviors encountered in long-term opioid therapy.[14]

**Critical nuance:** The CDC 2022 guideline and the APS/AAPM guideline both emphasize that aberrant behaviors have a **differential diagnosis** — they may reflect undertreated pain, psychiatric comorbidity, social stressors, or opioid use disorder, not exclusively misuse.[1][2] The 2022 CDC guideline specifically warns against using monitoring findings in a punitive manner.[4] Documenting the pattern is appropriate; interpreting it requires clinical assessment.

**Operational correction:** The tool should frame this as: "Frequent between-visit controlled substance contact is a behavioral signal warranting documentation and clinical assessment — not an automatic indicator of misuse." The differential should include inadequate pain control, anxiety, functional decline, and psychosocial crisis alongside misuse/diversion.

**Claim 4: Establishing a written protocol that controlled-substance questions are handled only at scheduled visits is appropriate**

**Verdict: Partially supported as a practice management strategy, but requires qualification.**

Controlled substance agreements (also called patient-provider agreements or opioid treatment agreements) are recommended by multiple professional societies and the Federation of State Medical Boards.[15][10][8][16] These agreements commonly include provisions about how and when prescriptions will be managed. The HIVMA/IDSA guideline describes the PPA as including a "plan of care" that specifies how treatment will be evaluated and modified.[16]

However, **no guideline specifically endorses restricting all controlled-substance communication to scheduled visits.** The CDC 2022 guideline emphasizes that follow-up should occur at least every 3 months and more frequently for higher-risk patients, and that telehealth may be appropriate in some settings.[1] A blanket policy refusing to address urgent controlled-substance concerns between visits could create safety risks (e.g., a patient in acute pain crisis, a patient experiencing withdrawal due to a pharmacy issue, or a patient reporting concerning symptoms).

**Operational correction:** The policy should be framed as: "Routine controlled-substance management decisions (refill timing, dose adjustments) are addressed at scheduled visits. Urgent clinical concerns will be triaged appropriately." This preserves the structure while avoiding a rigid policy that could delay necessary clinical responses.

---

**Claim 5: Schedule II opiates are not replaced for loss or theft as a matter of institutional policy (not DEA regulation)**

**Verdict: Correct characterization — this is institutional/practice policy, not a federal prohibition.**

There is **no federal DEA regulation that prohibits a prescriber from writing a new prescription for a Schedule II controlled substance to replace one that was lost or stolen.** Under 21 CFR Part 1306, a prescriber with a valid DEA registration may issue a new Schedule II prescription at their clinical discretion. The prohibition on "refills" for Schedule II substances (21 CFR 1306.12) means a pharmacy cannot dispense additional quantities on the same prescription, but a prescriber can write an entirely new prescription.[17][9]

The ACOEM Opioid Treatment Contract template includes the language: "I understand that lost, damaged, or stolen medication will NOT be replaced" — framed as a condition of the treatment agreement, not as a legal requirement.[10] This is a widely adopted institutional risk-management practice, not a regulatory mandate.

**Operational note:** The tool correctly identifies this as institutional policy. The PCP should understand that while replacement is legally permissible, most practice policies and controlled substance agreements prohibit it as a risk-mitigation measure. If a practice chooses to make an exception, it should be documented with clinical reasoning.

---

**Claim 6: Offering to help the patient file a police report when medication is reported stolen is reasonable practice**

**Verdict: Supported as reasonable practice, consistent with published treatment agreement templates.**

The ACOEM Opioid Treatment Contract explicitly states: "I agree to immediately report stolen opioid medication(s) to the police. My provider will also produce a police report if requested to do so".[10] This establishes a precedent for clinician involvement in the reporting process. Offering to assist with filing a police report serves multiple purposes: it creates an external record, it communicates the seriousness of the situation, and it may deter fabricated theft claims.

**Operational note:** This is a reasonable and defensible practice. The key point is that the police report is a documentation step, not a replacement trigger. The clinician should document the offer and the patient's response regardless of whether a report is filed.

---

**Claim 7: A single loss event is not disqualifying for future prescribing but should be noted and factored into the monitoring picture**

**Verdict: Supported by the general framework of graduated response to aberrant behaviors.**

The APS/AAPM 2009 guideline explicitly states that less serious aberrant behaviors (including isolated episodes) can be managed with education and enhanced monitoring rather than discontinuation.[2] The CDC 2022 guideline warns against dismissing patients or abruptly discontinuing opioids based on single concerning findings, emphasizing that doing so "can adversely affect patient safety".[1] The Delphi consensus study similarly found that experts agreed "stopping opioids is not important initially" when concerning behaviors arise, but that tapering or discontinuation may become appropriate if initial approaches fail.[14]

**Operational note:** This claim is well-aligned with current guidance. A single loss event should trigger: documentation of the event, review of the PDMP, possible UDS, a conversation about secure medication storage, and notation in the controlled substance agreement file. It should not automatically trigger discontinuation.

**Claim 1: Chronic non-cancer pain and OUD coexist more often than they are mutually exclusive; a patient can have both simultaneously.**

**Supported, with nuance.** The CDC 2022 Guideline explicitly states that "patients with co-occurring pain and substance use disorder require ongoing pain management that maximizes benefits relative to risks".[1] Studies using DSM-5 criteria in patients on long-term opioid therapy for pain find prevalence of **any OUD at 21–27%** and moderate-to-severe OUD at 4–9%.[1] A scoping review confirms that OUD "often co-occurs with chronic pain" and that assessment and treatment of these co-occurring disorders is complex.[2]

The phrase "more often than they are mutually exclusive" is directionally correct but could be misleading — the majority of chronic pain patients on opioids do not meet OUD criteria. The accurate framing is: co-occurrence is common enough that it should always be assessed, and the two conditions are not mutually exclusive. A key diagnostic nuance is that DSM-5 specifies tolerance and withdrawal should not count toward an OUD diagnosis if they occur solely in the context of opioids taken as prescribed.[3][4] Clinicians should use pain-adjusted diagnostic approaches to avoid over-diagnosing OUD in patients whose behaviors are driven purely by therapeutic intent.[3]

**Operational takeaway:** At the inherited-patient first visit, screen for OUD using DSM-5 criteria but apply the DSM-5 exclusion for tolerance/withdrawal occurring only in the context of prescribed use. Document the assessment explicitly.

**Claim 2: Signs of OUD alongside chronic pain include continued use despite clear harm, inability to adhere to prescribing structure despite genuine effort, loss of control over use, withdrawal symptoms driving use more than pain relief, and social/functional deterioration attributable to opioid use.**

**Partially supported — needs refinement.** The listed signs broadly map to DSM-5 criteria, but the claim conflates and oversimplifies them. The 11 DSM-5 criteria for OUD include: taking opioids in larger amounts or longer than intended, persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down, excessive time spent obtaining/using/recovering, craving, failure to fulfill role obligations, continued use despite social/interpersonal problems, giving up activities, use in physically hazardous situations, continued use despite physical/psychological problems caused or exacerbated by opioids, tolerance, and withdrawal.[1][4]

Key corrections:

- **"Inability to adhere to the agreed prescribing structure despite genuine effort"** is not a DSM-5 criterion per se. It may reflect "taking in larger amounts than intended" or "unsuccessful efforts to cut down," but the qualifier "despite genuine effort" is clinically important — it distinguishes compulsive use from intentional diversion or nonadherence for other reasons.
- **"Withdrawal symptoms driving use more than pain relief"** captures an important clinical observation but is not a standalone DSM-5 criterion. The DSM-5 withdrawal criterion counts only if withdrawal occurs outside the context of appropriate medical use. The concept of withdrawal-driven use overlapping with pain-driven use is precisely the diagnostic challenge that pain-adjusted instruments attempt to address.[4][5][3]
- Hallmarks specifically highlighted in the clinical literature for chronic pain patients include emotional volatility, taking more medication than prescribed, using opioids for reasons other than pain, frequent loss of medication, and early refills.[6]

**Operational takeaway:** Use the formal DSM-5 11-criterion checklist rather than an informal sign list. Document which criteria are met and whether behaviors are driven by therapeutic intent versus nontherapeutic motives. The AAFP summary of the CDC 2022 guideline and the VA/DoD guideline both provide structured approaches.[7][4]

**Claim 3: MOUD (buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone) significantly outperforms taper or discontinuation for patient outcomes including overdose survival.**

**Supported for buprenorphine and methadone; substantially weaker for naltrexone.** This claim should not treat all three MOUDs as equivalent.

Buprenorphine reduces all-cause mortality by approximately **60% (RR 0.34, 95% CI 0.26–0.45)**, and methadone by approximately **50% (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.41–0.54)**.[8] A large comparative effectiveness study found that only buprenorphine/methadone was associated with reduced overdose and opioid-related acute care use compared with no treatment; naltrexone, inpatient detoxification, and behavioral health interventions showed no significant benefit over no treatment during the 3-month follow-up.[9] Withdrawal management and medication tapers are associated with high relapse rates and increased mortality.[10][11]

Oral naltrexone is no more effective than placebo. Extended-release injectable naltrexone shows some benefit over no medication, but in intention-to-treat analyses it is inferior to buprenorphine due to induction barriers (patients must be opioid-free for 7–10 days).[8][10][12] A network meta-analysis confirmed that all pharmacotherapies had higher retention than control, with methadone ranked highest, followed by buprenorphine, then naltrexone.[13]

**Operational takeaway:** The claim is accurate for buprenorphine and methadone. When discussing MOUD with patients or in documentation, naltrexone should be presented as a second-line option with important caveats about induction difficulty and weaker mortality evidence. The decision-support tool should distinguish between the three agents.

**Claim 4: Buprenorphine for OUD can be initiated in primary care without DEA X-waiver requirements (post-MAT Act, 2023).**

**Supported.** The Mainstreaming Addiction Treatment (MAT) Act, enacted as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act signed December 29, 2022 (effective January 2023), eliminated the DATA-2000 X-waiver requirement.[14][15][16] Any practitioner with a standard DEA registration and Schedule III prescribing authority can now prescribe buprenorphine for OUD with **no patient caps**.[15][17] The only new requirement is that practitioners applying for a new or renewed DEA registration must complete **8 hours of training** on substance use disorders (self-attestation).[15]

One nuance: while the federal barrier is removed, **state-level regulations may still impose additional requirements** that are not aligned with the federal change.[7] The AAFP has noted that "many of us work under strict state requirements that are not aligned with this guidance".[7] Additionally, removing the X-waiver has increased the prescriber pool but has not yet translated into meaningful increases in patients receiving buprenorphine, suggesting that interest, comfort, and systems-level barriers remain the primary obstacles.[14][18][19]

**Operational takeaway:** The claim is federally accurate. The decision-support tool should prompt the PCP to verify their state's specific requirements and should note the 8-hour training attestation requirement for new/renewed DEA registrations. The tool should also provide practical initiation guidance, as the elimination of the X-waiver also eliminated the only structured OUD education many clinicians received.[14]

**Claim 5: Methadone for OUD requires an OTP; it cannot be prescribed for OUD from a primary care office.**

**Supported, with important exceptions.** Under current federal law, a DEA-registered prescriber **cannot prescribe methadone for OUD** to be filled at an outpatient pharmacy. Outpatient methadone for OUD can only be dispensed through SAMHSA-certified Opioid Treatment Programs (OTPs).[20][8][1] A 2025 JAMA Network Open table explicitly states that for primary care clinics (non-OTP): "No, not for methadone maintenance therapy for OUD".[20]

Key exceptions the decision-support tool should include:

- **72-hour rule:** Any clinician may administer (not prescribe) methadone for up to 72 hours to treat acute opioid withdrawal while arranging OTP referral. Under updated regulations, a 3-day supply may be dispensed at one time.[20][8][1]
- **Hospital exception:** Hospitalized patients can be initiated on or continued on methadone during inpatient stays.[8][1]
- **Methadone for pain:** Methadone can be prescribed by any DEA-registered clinician for a pain indication and filled at a retail pharmacy — but not for OUD.[20]
- **Evolving landscape:** There is active advocacy and policy discussion around allowing pharmacy-based methadone dispensing for OUD, though this is not yet law.[21]

**Operational takeaway:** The claim is correct for routine outpatient OUD treatment. The PCP should know about the 72-hour exception for bridging patients to an OTP and should not confuse methadone-for-pain prescribing authority with methadone-for-OUD authority.

**Claim 6: A patient with suspected/confirmed OUD cut off from prescribed opioids without a treatment plan is at acute, elevated overdose risk from illicit opioid use.**

**Strongly supported.** This is one of the best-evidenced claims in the set. A large retrospective cohort study found that discontinuing prescribed opioids was associated with increased overdose risk across all groups, with the highest risk among patients with OUD not receiving opioid agonist therapy (**AHR 3.18**, 95% CI 1.87–5.40).[22] A JAMA study of >113,000 patients found that opioid dose tapering was associated with significantly increased rates of overdose (aIRR 1.68) and mental health crisis (aIRR 2.28), with risk increasing at higher baseline doses and faster taper rates.[23]

“In a large cohort of patients in the US prescribed stable, long-term, higher-dose opioids, undergoing opioid dose tapering was associated with statistically significant risk of subsequent overdose and mental health crisis, including suicidality.”

The CDC 2022 Guideline states explicitly: "opioid therapy should not be discontinued abruptly" and warns that "patients prescribed opioids but unable to access ongoing care might be at risk for abrupt opioid discontinuation".[1] The FDA has issued a prescriber warning about hazards of rapid dose reduction including withdrawal, psychological distress, and suicidality.[1] The fentanyl-contaminated illicit supply adds a layer of lethality not present in earlier eras.[15]

**Operational takeaway:** This claim should be treated as a safety-critical principle. Document the overdose risk explicitly when inheriting a patient on chronic opioids, and ensure naloxone is prescribed. The CDC 2022 guideline also states: "Clinicians should not dismiss patients from their practice because of opioid use disorder because this can adversely affect patient safety".[1]

**Claim 7: Continuity of the existing opiate prescription with close monitoring while OUD evaluation and MOUD referral are pending is safer than discontinuation without a plan.**

**Supported by guideline consensus, though direct comparative trial evidence is limited.** The CDC 2022 Guideline (Recommendation 5) advises against abrupt discontinuation and recommends that clinicians "work closely with patients to optimize nonopioid therapies while continuing opioid therapy" when benefits outweigh risks.[1] The guideline further states that clinicians should "closely monitor patients who are unable to taper" and "work with patients to mitigate overdose risk".[1] The observational evidence on harms of discontinuation (discussed under Claim 6) provides the evidentiary basis for this approach.[22][23][1]

The nuance is that this is a **harm-reduction bridging strategy**, not an endorsement of indefinite continuation. The CDC guideline frames it as: continue while optimizing alternatives and arranging appropriate treatment.[1] Close monitoring should include PDMP checks, urine drug screening as clinically appropriate, shorter prescription intervals, and naloxone co-prescribing.[1]

**Operational takeaway:** Document the clinical rationale for continuing the prescription (e.g., "Continuing current opioid regimen at [dose] with [interval] monitoring to prevent abrupt discontinuation harms while OUD evaluation is completed and MOUD transition is arranged, per CDC 2022 Guideline Recommendation 5"). This protects both the patient and the clinician.

**Claim 8: The appropriate e-consult framing shifts from "please review pain management plan" to "patient with chronic pain and possible OUD — please advise on MOUD candidacy and transition plan."**

**Not directly addressed by any guideline**, but this is a reasonable operational recommendation consistent with the spirit of the CDC 2022 Guideline and ASAM guidance. The CDC guideline emphasizes that identification of OUD "represents an opportunity for a clinician to initiate potentially life-saving interventions" and that clinicians unable to provide treatment themselves "should arrange for patients with opioid use disorder to receive care from a substance use disorder treatment specialist".[1] The guideline also notes that for patients with active OUD not in treatment, clinicians "should consider buprenorphine or methadone treatment for opioid use disorder".[1]

The reframing is clinically sound because a pain management consult and an addiction medicine consult may go to different specialists, require different expertise, and generate different recommendations. Framing the consult around OUD and MOUD candidacy ensures the consultant addresses the most safety-critical question.

**Operational takeaway:** This is a best-practice recommendation rather than a guideline-mandated action. The decision-support tool should prompt the PCP to specify whether the consult question is pain management, OUD/MOUD evaluation, or both, and to route accordingly.

---

**Claim 9: OUD should not be framed as a moral failure in patient communication or documentation.**

**Strongly supported across all major guidelines and professional societies.** The CDC 2022 Guideline states that "opioid use disorder is a chronic, treatable disease from which persons can recover" and instructs clinicians to "discuss their concern with their patient in a nonjudgmental manner".[1] ASAM guidance emphasizes communicating "with compassion and respect and without judgment," noting that stigmatizing interactions "can drive people away from the care they need".[24][25] The Lancet review on OUD management states that "adopting a welcoming attitude that avoids risk of embarrassment or stigmatisation is crucial" and that "respecting the patient's decisions regarding their care without moral or paternalistic attitudes is paramount".[12] The AAFP notes that use of nonstigmatizing, person-first language has been shown to positively affect care, and that clinician stigma leads to under-treatment.[26]

An NEJM review frames OUD as involving neurobiological changes to striatocortical circuits, the extended amygdala, and prefrontal cortex — reinforcing the disease model over a moral framework.[27]

**Operational takeaway:** Use person-first language in documentation (e.g., "patient with opioid use disorder" rather than "opioid abuser" or "addict"). Avoid language implying volitional failure (e.g., "patient refuses to comply"). The NIDA provides a terminology guide that can be referenced for documentation standards.[26]
